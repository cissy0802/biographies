Biography: Ibn Battuta

1304 — c. 1368 · aged about 64
Moroccan judge · the longest traveller of the Middle Ages · the voice behind the Rihla
What is worth learning is not the seventy-three thousand miles. In a world with no passports, no remittances and no maps, what carried him was a set of credentials that could be verified anywhere he went — the credentials mattered more than the route. And the warning is this: those same credentials were also his ceiling.

◆Life in Brief

Born 24 February 1304 in Tangier, Morocco, into a family that had produced Maliki jurists for generations. In June 1325, aged twenty-one, he left alone on the hajj and did not come home for twenty-nine years: Egypt, Syria, Persia, East Africa, the Golden Horde, Central Asia, India, the Maldives, Ceylon, Bengal — and, by his own account, China. From 1332 he served eight years as a judge in the Delhi Sultanate; in 1342 he was sent as envoy to the Yuan court and lost ship, goods and party en route. He reached Fez in 1349, then crossed the Sahara to Mali in 1352. In 1354 the sultan ordered him to dictate his travels, which the man of letters Ibn Juzayy set down as a book. After his death around 1368 he was almost entirely forgotten, until European scholars translated him afresh in the nineteenth century.

◆Key Decisions: Not Waiting for the Caravan, Refusing the Shortest Route, Borrowing His Way into Delhi

14 June 1325: he did not wait for a caravan. The normal way from Tangier to Mecca was to wait for one of the great pilgrim caravans — safety in numbers. He did not wait (his own account of it is in the quotations below). He simply deleted the premise that one must travel in company — he was travelling inside the Muslim world, where the mosque, the madrasa and the Sufi lodge of every town along the way stood open to a young man who knew the law. The safety of company could be replaced by the hospitality of institutions.

1326, Alexandria: turning a closed task into an open-ended one. A pilgrimage has an end point: reach Mecca, go home. A Sufi shaykh told him he would meet his brothers in India, Sind and China. Whether or not the story is true, his subsequent behaviour is unambiguous: never take the shortest route; accept every detour that offers itself — after his first hajj he did not go home but turned toward Persia.

1332: borrowing heavily before entering Delhi. He had learned the rule of Sultan Muhammad ibn Tughluq's court — a gift to the sultan was repaid several times over. So he borrowed to buy camels, horses and valuables as his offering: a leveraged bet on the stability of a court custom. He won it and was made a judge of Delhi; he also lost it, leaving Delhi some 55,000 dinars in debt.

Sources: H. A. R. Gibb trans., The Travels of Ibn Battuta, A.D. 1325—1354 (Hakluyt Society), vol. 1 preface and vol. 3 on Delhi; Ross E. Dunn, The Adventures of Ibn Battuta (1986/2005), chs. 1 and 9.

◆The Turning Point: 1342, a Storm Knocks Him from Official Back to Traveller

The eight Delhi years were tense. Tughluq's court combined high pay with high mortality: the two things this king loved most were giving gifts and shedding blood. Suspected at one point of ties to a purged Sufi shaykh, he resigned in fear and spent five months fasting in a lodge.

In 1342 the sultan offered him a way out: carry a great mass of gifts to the Yuan court as ambassador — a dignified escape hatch. The embassy was first attacked by rebels near Delhi, where he was captured and escaped by hiding in a well; it barely reached Calicut on the southwest coast, where the fleet lay outside the harbour waiting on the wind. He went ashore that day for the Friday prayer, and the storm came. When he looked back, the ship carrying every gift, his retinue and an enslaved woman pregnant with his child was gone.

What matters is what he did next. Returning to Delhi empty-handed meant death — he had seen too much of how Tughluq handled failure. So he did not go back. He went south to the Maldives and started over on the strength of being "the judge from Delhi". The longest stretch of those twenty-nine years began with a refusal to go home and account for a failed mission.

Sources: Gibb trans., vols. 3—4; Ross E. Dunn, as above, chs. 10—11; Tim Mackintosh-Smith, Travels with a Tangerine (2001).

◆Character and Habits: Credentials Not Cargo, Three Contacts on Arrival, Never a Note

He was neither a merchant nor an explorer but a guest with papers. He never risked anything for trade, and he barely stepped outside the Muslim world. What he carried was a verifiable identity: training in Maliki law, a Tangier family of judges, and the transmissions he received from Sufi masters along the way — in every town the book records which shaykh he met. A Maghribi jurist was a scarce import in the East, which made those credentials unusually valuable.

His fixed routine on arriving: find the local judge, find the Sufi lodge, find a countryman — the three supplied, respectively, a post, board and lodging, and a guarantee of credit. He almost never slept rough.

He married where he landed and left when he left. He married repeatedly in Damascus, India and the Maldives, and as often divorced or simply departed, leaving children behind who are never mentioned again — marriage was both affection and the fastest interface into a local network. Money worked the same way: rich, robbed, then endowed again, and never accumulated.

The strangest habit of all: he kept no notes. What little he did record was lost when he was robbed off the Indian coast. The entire Rihla was dictated in his fifties from memories twenty and thirty years old — which explains both its astonishing density of detail and its rate of error.

Sources: Gibb trans., vols. 1—4; Ross E. Dunn, as above, chs. 2 and 13; Ibn Juzayy's closing note.

◆Controversies and Shadows: The Book May Not Be True, and the Man Was Not Broad-Minded

First, how much of the Rihla did he actually live? The long passages on Syria and Arabia closely resemble the twelfth-century travelogue of Ibn Jubayr, in places almost verbatim (Ibn Juzayy admits borrowing from predecessors). Several legs are doubted: the Chinese material is thin and plainly erroneous, and many scholars think he got no further than the southeastern coast. The book is a fifty-year-old's memory plus a professional writer's polish — not a ship's log. And the much-quoted line "travelling leaves you speechless, then turns you into a storyteller" appears in no version of the text; it is a modern fabrication.

Second, he was deeply implicated in slavery. He bought, received and gave away enslaved people all his life, women above all; the embassy to China included a hundred of them. He records this in the same tone he uses for buying horses or cloth — among the "property" lost in the 1342 wreck, the enslaved woman carrying his child stands in the same list as the cargo. This is not a footnote about the limits of his age; it was how he lived.

Third, his cosmopolitanism had a hard boundary. He crossed three continents, and the measure was always Maliki law. In the Maldives he ordered a thief's hand cut off (onlookers fainted) and tried to compel women to cover their upper bodies, finally admitting defeat. In Mali he could not accept that women went unveiled and mixed freely with men outside their families; he also complained to Mansa Sulayman's face that the hospitality was too thin — four months and nothing but bread, meat and sour milk. The world astonished him, and almost never changed him.

Sources: Ross E. Dunn, as above, introduction and appendix; Said Hamdun & Noël King, Ibn Battuta in Black Africa (1975/1994); Ralf Elger and others on the rihla genre; Gibb trans., vol. 4 on the Maldives.

◆What BigCat Can Take From This

His real asset was not courage but a set of credentials verifiable across borders: legal training, a chain of transmission, a family name. In a world with no central authority, those three let him be recognised and appointed twenty thousand kilometres from home. The same holds for the "AI super-individual": what is scarce is not capability itself but capability a stranger's system can verify fast — public work, a traceable record, people willing to vouch for you. Yet he never left the Muslim world, because past that boundary the credentials were worth nothing: a shrewd choice of route, and also the ceiling on what he could see. And one sharper point still: he kept no notes, so the richest first-hand experience of his century survives only as a reconstruction made thirty years later. Experience you do not record gets quietly rewritten inside your own head.

◆Questions Worth Sitting With

If Ibn Battuta were born today, what would he be?
Not a travel blogger — a blogger's asset is traffic, his was credentials. Closer to the professional who moves between international institutions and jurisdictions on a single mutually-recognised qualification, employable wherever they land. His gift was spotting and exploiting mutual recognition itself; today's equivalents are open-source reputation, verifiable public work, cross-platform identity. And the old problem would return unchanged: step outside the circle of recognition and the credentials drop to zero.
Were the conditions of his success reproducible?
The least reproducible part is the Muslim world of his century: from Morocco to the Indian Ocean it shared one body of law, one scholarly language, and a network of lodges and colleges that took travellers in. He was not hacking through wilderness; he was gliding along free infrastructure. Only three things transfer: turn your ability into a credential recognised elsewhere; on arriving anywhere new, connect to power, peers and countrymen first; and when a mission fails, do not assume you must go back and account for it.
What does his blind spot reveal?
He travelled twenty-nine years; his exposure grew and his standards did not move a notch. Faced with unveiled women in Mali, his first reaction was always "this is contrary to the law", never "why is it so here". Which suggests something counter-intuitive: exposure does not automatically produce openness. When someone travels carrying a framework that is complete, self-consistent, and keeps paying them in status and income, new experience is digested by the framework rather than revising it. What actually changes judgement are the moments the framework fails and costs you something — his never failed, so it never changed.
