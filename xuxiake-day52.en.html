Biographies: Xu Xiake

1587 — 1641 · died at 54
Ming-dynasty geographer · traveller · author of the Travel Diaries
What's worth learning isn't how far he walked. With no degree, no salary, no institution and no patron, he set himself a standard of evidence stricter than the official state gazetteers — and held to it for thirty years.

◆Life in Brief

Born in 1587 in Jiangyin, in a wealthy family that had opted out of officialdom: his father refused the imperial examinations for life, his mother wove cloth to keep the household running. He abandoned the exam track in his early twenties and spent the next three decades intermittently on the road, covering the territory of more than ten present-day provinces. His 1613 trip to Mount Tiantai produced the earliest surviving entry of the diaries; in autumn 1636, aged 51, he set out from Jiangyin on the "ten-thousand-li expedition," crossing Hunan, Guangxi and Guizhou into Yunnan over four years, reaching as far as Tengchong. By 1640 both feet had failed him and the Lijiang chieftain Mu Zeng sent men to carry him home; he died the following year. More than half the manuscript was lost. What generations of hands managed to recover became the Travel Diaries of Xu Xiake — roughly 400,000 characters today.

◆Key Decisions: quitting the exams, leaving at 51, turning travelogue into scholarship

First, quitting the imperial examinations in his early twenties. In the Ming, the exams were the only recognised ladder for an educated man; abandoning them meant abandoning any social identity at all. He had a precedent — his father never sat them — and a sentence of endorsement from his mother: "To set one's ambition on the four directions is a man's proper business." But no role called "traveller" existed. He traded something certain for something that did not yet have a name, and paid for it with a lifetime as a commoner, funding everything himself.

Second, the "ten-thousand-li expedition" of September 1636. For thirty years his trips had run a few months and he could turn back any time. Not this one: he was 51, the late-Ming southwest was in disorder, bandits, plague and river pirates were real probabilities, and he had no official standing to fall back on. Before leaving he settled every family matter — in effect arranging things on the assumption he might not return. A hobby pursued on and off for thirty years was upgraded, in a single move, into an irreversible project.

Third, after 1638 he rewrote "travelogue" as "textual scholarship." Tracing the Source of the River overturned the thousand-year consensus of the Tribute of Yu that the Yangtze rose in Mount Min, and identified the Jinsha as the true source. What won was not new data but a method: assert nothing you have not reached yourself. He named the earlier error precisely — geography inferred from political boundaries. The Min was "a river of China" in the central-plains field of view, the Jinsha lay beyond the pale, and so length gave way to orthodoxy.

Sources: Travel Diaries — Tracing the Source of the River; Chen Hanhui, "Epitaph for Xu Xiake"; Qian Qianyi, "Biography of Xu Xiake."

◆Turning Point — February 1637: a journey he could have ended with his dignity intact

At the end of the first lunar month of 1637 his boat, on the water near Hengyang, was boarded by river bandits at night. Everyone jumped overboard; baggage, travel money and books were all lost, and his companion, the monk Jingwen, took several blade wounds. He was 51, more than a thousand li from home, four months into the trip. Every condition pointed to the same face-saving conclusion: go home. He didn't. He borrowed from gentry along the route, sold what he had left, re-equipped and kept going south.

The harder part came next. Jingwen died that autumn in Nanning, asking that his ashes be carried to Mount Jizu in Yunnan; Xu Xiake carried them for nearly another year before arriving. This is usually told as a story of loyalty, but its real effect was structural: he converted a trip he could abandon at any moment into a promise he could not — the price of quitting went from embarrassment to betrayal. The most important survey work of his life came out of those two years of not being able to turn back.

Sources: Travel Diaries — the Hunan and Guangxi journals; Qian Qianyi, "Biography of Xu Xiake."

◆Character & Habits: written the same day, written only if seen, and into every cave himself

Written up daily, never left overnight. Whether he slept in a ruined temple, a cave or a poor village, the day's route was written up that day. The diary form was not a literary choice but an engineering one, against forgetting — place names, distances and bearings stop being reliable within a week.

Into the cave himself. He recorded over 270 caves, most of them entered personally with a torch in hand. Mayan Cave was said to hold dragons and demons, and no amount of money would buy him a companion; he went in alone and came out and wrote down the structure exactly as found. He measured depth and the run of strata by paces, body-lengths and rope — crude instruments, limited precision, but every number had a traceable origin.

Read against the official gazetteer. He carried the state-compiled Unified Gazetteer of the Great Ming with him and wrote down its errors plainly wherever the ground disagreed with the page — the single habit that turned the diaries from travel literature into checkable material.

An extremely high tolerance threshold. Plain clothes and straw sandals year-round, nights in temples and caves, wild fruit when the food ran out. In 1638 the servant who had followed him for years absconded with all his money; he walked the remaining two years alone.

Sources: Travel Diaries — the Hunan journal (Mayan Cave) and the Yunnan journal; Qian Qianyi, "Biography of Xu Xiake."

◆Controversy & Shadow: the concubine driven out, the calling cards and post-station warrants, and a famous line he never said

First, the accounts at home were never settled. He was away for years at a stretch while his wife and children carried the household. The sharpest item: his concubine Zhou, once pregnant, was driven out of the house by his principal wife, remarried into the Li family, and the son she bore took his stepfather's surname — Li Ji, whom Xu Xiake never acknowledged in his lifetime. The irony arrives afterwards: more than half the manuscript was scattered at his death, and one of the people who did most to hunt it down and copy it back together was that unacknowledged son.

Second, the "commoner traveller" image has preconditions. He travelled on family land income, gentry calling cards, monastery provisioning and the hospitality of local officials; across the southwest he repeatedly used post-station warrants gifted by officials to draw porters and horses, and it was the Lijiang chieftain Mu Zeng who finally had him carried home. He was independent of the system, but never independent of the network. Reading him as one man alone with a walking stick misses his actual talent: assembling a supply network fast in unfamiliar country.

Third, reliability is uneven — and the most famous line is fake. The diaries do pass on hearsay he never verified, and his accounts of the southwest's ethnic minorities carry the prejudices of his class. The best-known line attributed to him — "return from the Five Sacred Peaks and you see no mountains; return from Huangshan and you see no peaks" — is not his; later hands spun it out of his remark that after Huangshan there is nothing left to see. A man who built his life on writing only what he saw is best known for a sentence someone else wrote for him.

Sources: the Travel Diaries and later editorial prefaces; records associated with Li Ji's collected works; Qian Qianyi, "A Letter Urging the Diaries into Print."

◆What BigCat Can Take From This

What separated him from the countless other travellers of his century was not distance but recording specification: written every day, in one consistent format, restricted to what a later reader could check — bearings, distances, landform shape. For a private project to become an asset, what does the work is not the experience itself but the layer of structure that makes the experience verifiable. Second: "assert nothing you have not reached yourself" is a discipline against hallucination, and marking the difference between what you verified and what you merely read is the line most worth holding when working with a model. The counter-lesson matters as much: he left no institution, no successor and no copy, over half his output scattered after his death, surviving only through a few accidental private relays. Structure you don't build is thirty years wagered on luck.

◆Questions Worth Sitting With

If Xu Xiake were born today, who would he be?
Probably not a geographer — satellite imagery has pushed the marginal value of "being there" close to zero. His real capability was setting himself a standard of evidence stricter than any institution's and never once breaking it in thirty years, and the closest position today is the long-horizon independent recorder: fieldwork, first-hand evaluation, sustained observation of one narrow domain. The big difference is distribution. His manuscript survived by human relay; copying now costs nothing, so the problem inverts — not how to survive, but how what survives gets found and trusted.
Which of his conditions cannot be reproduced?
Three were all necessary: land income that removed the need to earn a living, two generations of family tolerance for staying out of officialdom, and the quasi-free infrastructure of monastery and gentry networks. The first two were luck — he never had to choose between supporting a household and leaving — and that alone eliminates most imitators at step one. The third he built himself: the supply network in unfamiliar country was assembled route by route. That is the reproducible part — treat the resource network as part of the project rather than as an obstacle to it.
What does his blind spot reveal?
A man who recorded the outside world down to the day left almost nothing about his own household: Zhou's expulsion and Li Ji's birth appear nowhere in his hand. This isn't hypocrisy so much as the cost built into a direction of attention — extreme precision aimed outward tends to be paid for in coarseness aimed inward, and the person never notices, because omissions leave no trace. The inference holds for anyone with a long obsession: the question worth asking periodically is not "am I doing this well enough" but "which whole class of things have I systematically kept out of the record?"
