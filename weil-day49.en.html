Biography: Simone Weil

1909 — 1943 · died at 34
French philosopher · a scholar who put herself on the factory floor · a mystic who refused baptism
She argued that attention is the highest capacity a human being can reach — and used the same absolutism to burn herself out at 34. Take her method; do not take her ending.

◆Life in Brief

Born in Paris in 1909 to a prosperous, thoroughly secular Jewish family; her father was a doctor and her brother André Weil became one of the century's great mathematicians. In 1928 she entered the École Normale Supérieure ranked first in the entrance exam (Simone de Beauvoir came second), qualified to teach philosophy in 1931, and was posted to provincial schools, where leading unemployed workers through the streets earned her the local press nickname "the Red Virgin." What followed was a sequence of deliberate descents: at the end of 1934 she took leave to work as a factory hand; in 1936 she went to the front of the Spanish Civil War; in 1938 a religious experience turned her toward Christianity — and she refused baptism for the rest of her life. In 1942 she fled to New York, then travelled alone to London to work for the Free French. She died in England on 24 August 1943, having published almost nothing in book form.

◆Key Decisions: Demoting Herself, Going to the Front, Refusing Safety

First, in December 1934, she broke off an academic career to work piece-rate on a factory floor. She was already a tenured philosophy teacher who had written a good deal of theory about the labour movement — and concluded she was "speaking on behalf of people I do not know." She took a year's leave, concealed her credentials, and on 4 December started as a press operator at the Alsthom electrical works in Paris, later moving to the Carnaud canning plant and then Renault. Frail and slow-handed, she chronically missed her quotas, lost wages, and was fired at one point; her Factory Journal logs the daily piece counts, the hours, and the moments of being shouted at by a foreman. What she brought back was not sympathy but a cold finding: sustained fatigue and humiliation destroy the capacity to think first — people lose their thoughts before they lose their capacity to resist.

Second, in August 1936, she went to Spain. Severely short-sighted and barely able to handle a rifle, she joined the anarchist Durruti Column anyway and insisted on the Aragon front. A month in, she stepped into a pot of boiling oil sunk in the ground, was badly burned, and was taken away by her parents — an accident that probably saved her, since her unit was almost wiped out soon after. What changed her was what she saw: in a 1938 letter to the writer Georges Bernanos she described volunteers who set out full of sacrifice and landed in a war crueller than one fought by mercenaries, and recorded a fifteen-year-old Falangist boy shot simply for refusing to change sides. She never again believed that "the right side" is exempt from violence.

Third, at the end of 1942, she gave back the safety she had already secured. Having reached New York, she decided staying was a betrayal and made her own way to London, where the plan she submitted to the Free French was a corps of front-line nurses to treat the wounded under fire — with an expected casualty rate that was enormous. It was, in essence, a demand for her share of the danger. De Gaulle read it and said only, "But she's mad." The plan was refused; she was put to writing documents and produced The Need for Roots.

Sources: Simone Weil, La Condition ouvrière (containing the Factory Journal, 1951); letter to Georges Bernanos (1938); Simone Pétrement, Simone Weil: A Life (1973).

◆Turning Point: Solesmes, 1938 — the Strongest Faith Experience, Paired with the Strongest Institutional Doubt

During Holy Week 1938 she spent ten days at the abbey of Solesmes listening to Gregorian chant, in the worst phase of the migraines that plagued her whole life. Every note, she later wrote, landed like a blow on her skull — and yet through "extreme attention" she could get outside the suffering body. There she was given George Herbert's short seventeenth-century poem "Love (III)," which she recited over and over like a prayer; during one such recitation, in the words of her own Spiritual Autobiography, "Christ himself came down and took possession of me." Until then she had never regarded religious experience as real.

The other half of the turn matters more: for the five years that followed she still refused baptism. Her reasons are in her letters to Father Perrin — the Church draws a line with its Anathema sit, and she would not step inside it and leave the pagans, the atheists and the unbelievers outside. Any collective that demands belonging she called "the Great Beast": the moment it asks for loyalty, it has already done its members' thinking for them. She held on to the intensity of the experience and to her suspicion of the institution at once, letting neither consume the other.

Sources: Simone Weil, Attente de Dieu (1950) — the Spiritual Autobiography and the letters to Fr. J.-M. Perrin.

◆Character and Habits: Attention as a Trainable Organ

She indexed her income to the poorest. While teaching, she cut her take-home pay to the level of the local unemployment benefit and handed the rest to workers' organisations and strike funds; convalescing in London in 1943, she ate no more per day than the ration allowed in occupied France.

She believed concentration accumulates through "useless" schoolwork. In her essay on school studies she argued that a geometry problem you never solve is valuable not for the answer but for the stretch of unrewarded attention you sustained on it — and that this capacity transfers to any object, including a suffering person.

She learned languages to avoid translations. She read Plato daily in Greek and began Sanskrit around 1940 solely to read the Bhagavad Gita directly. She was also profoundly clumsy, and made no effort to hide it: myopic, uncoordinated, never without a cigarette, always last in factory output and needing colleagues to finish her batches. Her teacher Alain nicknamed her "the Martian."

She did not write to publish. Gravity and Grace, The Need for Roots and Waiting for God exist as notebooks, letters and internal reports, all edited into books by others after her death — she wrote only to think something through.

Sources: Attente de Dieu, "Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies"; Simone Pétrement, Simone Weil: A Life; the Cahiers notebooks.

◆Controversy and Shadow: Her Writing on Jews, Death by Refusing Food, Actions That Failed

First, her writing on Judaism is the deepest fracture in her reputation. Born into a Jewish family, she systematically denied any continuity between Judaism and Christianity and read the Old Testament as a record of collective violence. After the Vichy regime issued its Statute on Jews in 1940, she wrote to the Minister of Public Education, arguing in a tone close to irony that she did not know what "Jew" meant and that her own tradition was French and Christian. Critics call it a textbook case of self-hatred; whatever reading one takes, these pages were written in the years her own people were being sent to their deaths, and they are hard to look at.

Second, her death was not a romantic martyrdom. Diagnosed with tuberculosis in April 1943 and told to eat more, she refused to exceed the occupied-zone ration and refused most treatment. She died at a sanatorium in Ashford on 24 August; the coroner's verdict was suicide by refusing to eat while the balance of her mind was disturbed. Treating suffering as the way to connect with others ended by making her unable to do anything for anyone — a mind that stops producing at 34 is a net loss.

Third, nearly every action she initiated failed. A year in the factories bought permanently damaged health and one journal; a month in Spain, and her only wound was self-inflicted; the nurses' corps was rejected; the parachute mission back into France never happened. Susan Sontag put it bluntly in 1963: we read an author like this for her seriousness, not because we actually agree with her positions. Her method is enormously valuable; her life plan is not to be copied.

Sources: Susan Sontag, "Simone Weil," New York Review of Books, February 1963; Ashford coroner's report (1943).

◆Lines Worth Keeping

  • "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity." — letter to the poet Joë Bousquet, 13 April 1942
  • "Absolutely unmixed attention is prayer." — Gravity and Grace (1947), "Attention and Will"
  • "Force is that x that turns anybody who is subjected to it into a thing." — opening of "The Iliad, or the Poem of Force" (1939), Mary McCarthy's translation
  • "There I received forever the mark of a slave." — Spiritual Autobiography, in Waiting for God, on her year in the factories
  • "A right is not effectual by itself, but only in relation to the obligation to which it corresponds." — opening of The Need for Roots (1949)

◆What BigCat Can Take Away

The most transferable thing she offers is treating attention as a resource that is trainable, transferable, and systematically destroyed by environment. Three of her judgements hold up line by line today: the quality of attention sets the ceiling on cognition, and deep concentration can be accumulated deliberately on low-return tasks — a problem you fail to solve trains you no less than one you solve; the great enemy of concentration is not laziness but fatigue and humiliation; and insisting on primary sources over second-hand paraphrase is worth more, not less, in an era where information passes through layer after layer of rewriting. Second is her wariness of collectives: the moment an organisation asks for belonging it starts doing your judging for you — and her answer was not to leave but to stand on the boundary, participating without signing. Third is the counter-example: treating self-consumption as moral proof is a path that drives output to zero.

◆Questions Worth Sitting With

If Weil were alive inside today's attention economy, what would she become?
Her central claim — that attention is the highest moral capacity — has been cashed out in reverse by today's business models: an entire industry earns its revenue by dismantling it. She would probably be its fiercest critic and might also be its most effective practitioner: long single-object concentration, primary sources over paraphrase, refusing to let an algorithm define what you belong to, amount to an executable personal protocol. The real risk is that she would once again turn critique into self-punishment, treating non-use as the only clean posture.
Was the factory year a necessary price, or could it have been bought more cheaply?
The insight she paid for with her health — that fatigue and humiliation destroy thought before they destroy resistance — only becomes real in the body; reading about it does not deliver it. But the price need not have been that high: a few months instead of a year, medical care kept, and the conclusion would likely be the same. What transfers is first-hand sampling rather than total sacrifice: take a shift on the front line, use the product you built, walk one process end to end. Sampling is meant to bring you back with a finding; sacrifice often does not bring you back.
What kind of blind spot does her wholesale rejection of her Jewish identity reveal?
Her intellectual weapon was suspicion of all collective power, and that weapon lost its calibration the moment she turned it on her own origins. The sharpest critical framework goes out of control precisely where it feels most natural to apply. The timing matters more still: these pages were written in the years her own people were being systematically murdered, which shows that a moral judgement resting only on logical consistency can be completely blind to a concrete situation.
