Biography: Nelson Mandela

1918 — 2013 · aged 95
first democratically elected president of South Africa · political prisoner for 27 years · Nobel Peace Prize, 1993
What is worth learning is not forgiveness. He treated reconciliation as a piece of engineering to be completed while the enemy still held the guns: dismantle their fear first, then press your own victory. And the warning is this: the same man who read political timing almost perfectly was a decade slow on his family and on AIDS.

◆Life in Brief

Born 18 July 1918 in Mvezo, Transkei, into a junior branch of the Thembu royal house. In 1941 he fled an arranged marriage for Johannesburg; in 1944 he founded the ANC Youth League with Walter Sisulu and Oliver Tambo, and in 1952 opened South Africa's first Black law firm. When every legal channel of protest was closed off in 1961 he went underground and founded the armed wing Umkhonto we Sizwe. Arrested in 1962, sentenced to life in 1964 — eighteen years on Robben Island, nine more in two other prisons. Released on 11 February 1990, sworn in as president in 1994, he handed over power voluntarily in 1999 after a single term.

◆Decisive Choices: Open Fire, Refuse Release, Negotiate Alone

1961: abandoning nonviolence. After the Sharpeville massacre of 1960 (police fired on a dispersing crowd; 69 dead) the ANC was banned. His judgement was not moral but situational: once lawful protest is itself made unlawful, staying nonviolent hands the other side every decision. The first sabotage on 16 December struck installations only and avoided lives — restrained violence, meant to keep a later negotiation possible. It cost him 27 years in prison, and he stayed on the American terrorism watch list until 2008.

February 1985: refusing to walk out. President Botha publicly offered to release him if he renounced violence as a political instrument. He was 66 and had served 22 years. He refused. He saw the structure of the deal: to accept release was to concede that he was an individual who had erred, rather than the representative of an organisation that had been crushed. What he wanted was not freedom but to leave as a different person.

From November 1985: going around his own organisation. After prostate surgery he was held alone, and he read the isolation as an opportunity — he could probe the other side without implicating anyone else. He wrote to the justice minister, Kobie Coetsee; dozens of secret meetings followed over four years, ending with Botha at Tuynhuys in July 1989. Kathrada, in the same prison, objected strongly; Tambo wrote from Lusaka demanding to know what he was doing. His defence: these were talks about talks, and if it went wrong the blame was his alone. Lose the bet and he was a traitor; win it and 1990 became possible.

Sources: Nelson Mandela, Long Walk to Freedom (1994), parts 9—10; Anthony Sampson, Mandela: The Authorised Biography (1999), ch. 20—24; Ahmed Kathrada, Memoirs (2004).

◆Turning Points: Twice He Spoke to the Enemy's Face

20 April 1964, Pretoria. Rivonia was a capital case, and the standard defence was to testify, rebut point by point and argue for a lesser sentence. He and his lawyers did the opposite: he did not testify, and instead made a statement from the dock, immune to cross-examination — throwing away the legally optimal move to buy one uninterrupted, complete address. He spoke for nearly three hours, then took off his glasses, looked at the judge and finished with the line about being prepared to die. The court was silent for about thirty seconds. An unwinnable case became an international event.

10 April 1993, Johannesburg. Chris Hani, the ANC's military leader, was shot dead in his own driveway by a white far-right gunman, and the country stood on the edge of civil war. Mandela was not yet president, but it was he, not de Klerk, who went on national television. The core of the address was not condemnation but a precise pairing: a white man full of hatred killed Hani; a white Afrikaner neighbour risked her life to take down the number plate, which is why the assassin was caught. He refused to let the event be read as race against race. Four days later the date of the first election was set.

Sources: Rivonia trial record, 20 April 1964; SABC national address, 10 April 1993; Sampson, as above, ch. 14 and 30.

◆Character and Habits: The Enemy's Language, a Garden, Speaking Last

Up at 3.30 a.m., running in place in his cell for forty-five minutes, then a hundred fingertip press-ups, two hundred sit-ups and fifty deep knee-bends — Monday to Thursday, resting at weekends. After release he still exercised at dawn and made his own bed, hotels included.

In prison he learned Afrikaans and read Afrikaner poetry and history, which more than a few fellow prisoners saw as consorting with the enemy. His reasoning was purely practical: to speak a man's own language is to speak straight into his heart. Every later contact with warders and ministers rested on that unglamorous investment.

He secured a narrow strip of ground against the wall and grew vegetables — tomatoes, onions, chillies — and shared them with the warders, using gardening to explain leadership: the seed, the patience, knowing when to leave it alone. In 1976 the buried manuscript of Long Walk to Freedom was dug up in that garden, and he lost his study privileges for four years.

He spoke last in meetings. This came from the tribal councils of his boyhood at Mqhekezweni: everyone spoke, and the chief summed up at the end. So he let disagreement surface in full first, then offered a formulation in which everyone was included, rather than stating a position and recruiting to it.

Sources: Long Walk to Freedom, parts 2 and 9; Richard Stengel, Mandela's Way (2010); Kathrada, as above.

◆Controversy and Shadow: Family, AIDS, and His Loyalties

First, the heaviest cost fell on his family. His first wife, Evelyn, accused him of violence in the marriage (he denied it); they divorced in 1958. His mother died in 1968 and his eldest son Thembi in a car crash in 1969; both requests to attend the funerals were refused. His daughter Maki told him to his face, once grown, that he had never had time for them. His handling of Winnie is harder still: her Mandela United Football Club killed the fourteen-year-old Stompie Seipei in 1989 and she was convicted of kidnapping in 1991, yet he shielded her for years and only removed her as a deputy minister in 1995.

Second, his failure on AIDS. HIV infection rose steeply in South Africa during his presidency (1994—1999) while almost all his political capital went into racial reconciliation; he later called this his greatest regret. He spoke out seriously only after leaving office: in 2005, when his eldest surviving son Makgatho died of AIDS, he held a press conference to name the cause and break the silence. He was ten years late, and in those ten years South Africa lost over a million people.

Third, loyalty outranked principle. He publicly embraced Gaddafi, Castro and Suharto on the grounds that they had backed the ANC when it was most isolated; visiting Libya in 1997 he said those who objected to his going were "without morals". In economics, after Davos in 1992 he dropped the Freedom Charter's commitment to nationalisation, and in 1996 adopted the austerity-leaning GEAR programme: political equality arrived, the structure of wealth barely moved, and South Africa remains one of the most unequal countries on earth.

Sources: Sampson, as above, ch. 26 and 31—34; Truth and Reconciliation Commission report, vol. 2; press conference of 6 January 2005.

◆What BigCat Can Take From This

The most transferable move is to deal with the other side's fear before pressing your own aim: learning Afrikaans, keeping the old government's civil servants, wearing the Springbok jersey. None of that is generosity; it is the removal of the assumption "you are about to lose everything" from an opponent's head, which is what makes a settlement possible at all. Collaboration works the same way: people who oppose you often do not disagree with the conclusion — they are defending against a loss no one has said out loud. The second move is the 1985 refusal: seeing what identity a benefit comes attached to matters more than seeing the benefit. And then the sharp one — his reading of political timing was near-perfect, while his family and AIDS were a decade behind. Concentration at that intensity generates its own blind spot, and the blind spot is usually not inside the work but outside it; it never raises an alarm, it quietly compounds.

◆Questions Worth Sitting With

If Mandela were born today, could he still become Mandela?
One of his key assets was twenty-seven years of silence: nobody could see him, so everyone projected their own hopes onto a symbol. Today the unmandated secret talks of 1985 could hardly stay secret a fortnight, and once leaked, the charge of "sell-out" would set before he could explain. His method depended on an environment where you could act first and account for it later; the social-media era suits people who take positions, not people who negotiate.
Are the conditions of his success reproducible?
Mostly not. The apartheid state was already squeezed on three sides — international sanctions, economic decline, the end of the Cold War — and the white elite needed a dignified way out; Black South Africans were eighty per cent of the population, so time was on his side. What he did was steer a deadlock the other side already wanted out of towards a bloodless settlement. Only the method transfers: refuse to have your position redefined as a personal fault; deal first with what the opponent fears most; hand over power once you have won.
What do his blind spots reveal?
They fall almost entirely in domains that are not adversarial — family, public health, economic structure. His lifelong training was struggle and negotiation: identify the opponent, weigh the balance of forces, find the terms of exchange. AIDS offers no counterparty, only epidemiology and allocation; a family is not a contest to win. The general risk: a person's sharpest capability will redescribe every problem as the kind it is good at. Anything that cannot be translated into "negotiation" is automatically demoted — not because he did not care, but because his framework left it no place.
