Biography: Katherine Johnson

1918 — 2020 · died at 101
NASA computer · mathematician · "hidden figure"
What is worth learning is not how accurate her arithmetic was. Through the one decade in which the machines had to be used and could not yet be trusted, she made herself the only means of verifying them.

◆Life in Brief

Born in White Sulphur Springs, West Virginia, in 1918. The local school for Black children stopped at eighth grade, so her father moved the family 200 km away purely so his children could attend high school. She finished high school at 14 and college at 18, majoring in mathematics and French. In June 1953, aged 34, she joined the West Area Computing unit at NACA's Langley laboratory — a pool of women doing calculations, staffed along segregated lines. Over the next 33 years she computed Shepard's suborbital trajectory, Glenn's orbital insertion equations and Apollo's lunar rendezvous paths, was credited on 26 reports, retired in 1986, and died in 2020 at 101.

  • 1953.06Joins West Area Computing at Langley. Two weeks later she is loaned "temporarily" to the Flight Research Division — and never goes back.
  • 1958NACA becomes NASA and West Area Computing is dissolved: the segregated unit goes, the promotion ceiling stays.
  • 1960.09Co-authors Technical Note D-233 — the first woman credited as an author in the Flight Research Division.
  • 1961.05.05Shepard's Freedom 7 suborbital flight; she computed the trajectory.
  • 1962.02.20Before orbital insertion, Glenn asks specifically for her to check the IBM 7090's numbers by hand.
  • 1969–70Apollo 11 lunar rendezvous trajectories; after the Apollo 13 accident the crew flies home on backup procedures she helped design.
  • 2015.11.24Receives the Presidential Medal of Freedom at 97. Hidden Figures is published and filmed the following year.

◆Key Decisions: taking the place, staying in the temporary job, fighting for the byline

First, in 1939 she took the "integration slot." The Supreme Court had ruled the year before that states must provide equal graduate education for Black students; West Virginia's governor picked three people to send to the state university, and she was the only woman — a job with no precedent and no protection. She studied for a single session, left when she became pregnant, and never held a graduate degree. The cost was concrete: inside an institution that defined credentials by the doctorate, she spent the next 33 years as the person who did the calculations, never the person who set the problems.

Second, in 1953 she stayed put in a temporary posting. Two weeks into the job she was loaned to the all-white, all-male Flight Research Division and was supposed to go back. She didn't — Pearson, the section head, never raised it, and Vaughan, who ran West Computing, never asked for her return. She then asked to attend the editorial meetings and was told women didn't go to those. Her reply: is there a law against it? There wasn't, so she went. From then on she sat in the room where the questions were framed, rather than waiting for them to arrive at her desk.

Third, in 1960 she refused to hand over the credit. Before transferring to Houston, her collaborator Skopinski told their supervisor the report should be finished by her — she had done the work anyway. That September the technical note on satellite burnout azimuth (TN D-233) was published under both names. This was not a vanity point: at NASA an authored report was the only contribution the institution accounted for. Everything else was booked to "the group."

Sources: Margot Lee Shetterly, Hidden Figures (2016); NASA Technical Note D-233 (1960); NASA's official biography and her 2008 WHRO oral history.

◆Turning Point — February 1962: the astronaut didn't trust the machine, he trusted one person

In February 1962, Friendship 7 was days from launch. For the first time the orbital numbers came from an IBM 7090 running on a linked system at Goddard and Bermuda — something entirely new, and something that had just produced errors. Glenn's request was relayed by the engineers present as: "Get the girl to check the numbers. If she says the numbers are good, I'm ready to go." She took a Friden desktop mechanical calculator and worked through the machine's own procedure point by point, over a day and a half. The numbers matched, Glenn launched and came home after three orbits. That day and a half changed not the mission but her position: from the person who checked, to the person asked for by name.

That position existed inside a very narrow window: computers had become strong enough to be necessary and were not yet strong enough to be trusted. She was irreplaceable not because she was faster than the machine — she was far slower — but because she could reproduce its conclusion along a completely independent path. The window closed during the 1970s, and she spent the next forty years in obscurity.

Sources: Shetterly, Hidden Figures (2016); NASA's official biography, "Katherine Johnson: The Girl Who Loved to Count."

◆Character & Habits: count everything, then ask until the other person gives up

Compulsive counting. By her own account she counted the steps to the road, the dishes and the pieces of silverware she washed — "anything that could be counted, I did." This is not a charming childhood anecdote: her entire method was to break a continuous process into countable discrete steps and check them one by one.

Solve it geometrically; don't approximate numerically. For the Glenn check she did not imitate the machine's iterative algorithm — she went back to analytic geometry, the subject her college mentor Claytor had created a course in for her alone. Handed a new problem, her first move was to find a formulation with a closed-form solution, not to tune parameters until they converged.

Relentless questions. She kept asking "why is it done this way," "what does that symbol mean," until the engineers simply put her on the meeting list. Her own summary: in those days a woman had to be assertive, even aggressive.

Two timelines in parallel. Her husband James Goble died of a brain tumour in 1956; she raised three daughters alone while working full time, remarried in 1959, and sang in the same church choir for fifty years.

Sources: NASA's official biography; 2008 WHRO oral history; Shetterly, Hidden Figures (2016).

◆Controversy & Shadow: an invented famous scene, a hardship she herself denied, and the hundreds crowded out by one

First, part of her iconic image rests on fiction. The most famous scene in the 2016 film — the near-kilometre round trip to a "colored" restroom, the white supervisor smashing the segregation sign with a crowbar — did not happen. Johnson said repeatedly that she used the nearest restroom and nobody challenged her. The sign-smashing supervisor is an invented composite: a quiet transgression Black women carried out themselves, rewritten as a white man's heroic moment.

Second, her own playing-down of segregation got put to other uses. She often said "I didn't feel the segregation, because everybody there was doing research." Yet in the same years a "COLORED COMPUTERS" sign stood on the West Computing table, and her colleague Miriam Mann took it away into her handbag again and again until it stopped reappearing. A survivor's equanimity is hers to have, but the line has since been quoted endlessly as evidence that the barriers were never really that high.

Third, the individual-hero story crowds out an entire cohort and hides the fact that the institution did not change. Langley employed hundreds of women computers, most of them still nameless; Dorothy Vaughan, who ran West Computing, lost her supervisory role when the unit dissolved in 1958 and never managed anyone again; Johnson herself never entered management in 33 years. Her recognition arrived at 97, and only because a bestseller and a film cashed it in — by then most of her contemporaries were dead.

Sources: Shetterly, Hidden Figures (2016); NASA and WHRO interview transcripts; US press fact-checks of the 2016 film's historical liberties.

◆What BigCat Can Take From This

She offers the earliest template for human-machine collaboration, and the conclusion is counter-intuitive: in the phase where a machine is already strong enough to be necessary but not yet strong enough to be trusted, the value is not in computing faster but in being able to reproduce its conclusion along an independent path. Large-model output sits in exactly that position today — the scarce skill is not using it, it is verifying it independently. Second, the fight over the byline: doing the work is not the same as being credited for it, and institutions only recognise attributable traces (reports, commits, design docs). Anything left with "the group" is left nowhere. Third, that question — is there a law against it? — treats an unwritten norm as a hypothesis to be falsified. The downside is worth recording too: build a career on a skill that will eventually be mechanised, and the scarcity disappears on its own.

◆Questions Worth Sitting With

If Katherine Johnson were born today, who would she be?
Her core ability was turning a vague problem into a closed-form formulation and then verifying it point by point — the nearest roles today are formal verification, numerical methods or model evaluation. But those posts gate on a graduate degree, which is precisely what she lacked; she got through that door because Langley in 1953 needed a great deal of human calculation and did not require credentials for it. Someone of equal ability today might be filtered out before the interview. The question is: how does an organisation find the people without the credential?
Glenn's "get the girl to check the numbers" — luck, or inevitability?
Inevitability embedded in luck. The inevitable part: she had spent seven years making herself the only person in the Flight Research Division who could recompute an orbit independently, with an authored report to prove it. The luck: five years earlier there was no machine to check, ten years later nobody would have doubted one. What is reproducible is making yourself the independent verifier; what is not is that the interval of technical uncertainty happened to open in front of her. Conversely, AI is now manufacturing such windows in bulk.
She said "I didn't feel the segregation." What does that blind spot represent?
Contracting attention entirely onto the problem in front of you is a highly effective survival strategy under pressure — it kept her producing for 33 years. But the same strategy left her unable to see the system-level fact: she personally got through, the institution did not move an inch, and hundreds of colleagues remain nameless. Individual adaptation and structural failure can both be true at once, and the person inside usually sees only the first. The inference: one person's experience of passing through a system is not evidence that the system can be passed through.
