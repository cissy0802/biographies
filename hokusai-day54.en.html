Biographies:
Katsushika Hokusai

1760 — 1849 · died at 88
ukiyo-e artist · Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji · "the old man mad about painting"
The lesson isn't "late bloomer." He turned starting over into a standing procedure — thirty-odd changes of art name, ninety-three changes of address, and at seventy-four a public declaration that everything he made before seventy was worthless. His masterworks came after he decided to void himself.

◆Life in Brief

Born in 1760 in the Katsushika district of Edo (today's Sumida, Tokyo), adopted as a child into the Nakajima family, mirror-makers to the shogunate, and apprenticed young to a lending library and then a woodblock carver — the first thing he learned was how to reproduce someone else's line. In 1778 he entered the studio of Katsukawa Shunshō to make actor prints; after Shunshō died in 1793 he left the school and went off to study the Kanō and Rinpa traditions and Western perspective. Around 1798 he took the name Hokusai, after Myōken, the deity of the North Star he worshipped. From 1814 he issued the Hokusai Manga; around the age of seventy he completed Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji. His studio burned in 1839, and his last years went into one-off brush paintings. He died in Edo on 10 May 1849.

◆Key Decisions: leaving the brand, selling the workbook, betting on a new pigment at seventy

First: leaving the Katsukawa school around 1794. He was thirty-four or thirty-five, a signed actor-print artist with steady commissions. Edo painting ran on schools, and leaving one meant losing the brand and the distribution at the same time. He left anyway, to study the Kanō tradition — regarded as heresy inside the ukiyo-e world — and Western perspective, and spent the next few years selling chili peppers and calendars in the street. What he swapped out wasn't an employer; it was his own technical stack.

Second: in 1814, turning a private teaching aid into a public product. The Hokusai Manga began as sketch models for out-of-town pupils: figures, ghosts, buildings, water patterns, plants and animals — thousands of parts. He handed it to the Nagoya bookseller Eirakuya Tōshirō to publish openly, and it ran to fifteen volumes and nearly four thousand drawings. Short term it was giving away the trade secret; long term that parts library became his most widely circulated asset.

Third: around 1830, at seventy, betting on a new material. The blue that makes Thirty-Six Views hit so hard is not a traditional plant pigment but Prussian blue, freshly imported from Europe and only just cheap enough — dense, stable, printable in graded layers, where the old dayflower blue faded. "Hokusai blue" isn't an innate eye; it's a veteran's adoption decision about a material that had only just become available and had no track record.

Sources: Iijima Kyoshin, Katsushika Hokusai den (1893); Timothy Clark, ed., Hokusai: Beyond the Great Wave (2017).

◆Turning Points: the giant sheet of 1817, the fire of 1839

First: October 1817, Nagoya. In a temple courtyard he laid out paper the size of about a hundred and twenty tatami mats, used a broom for a brush and a whole tub of ink for pigment, and painted a colossal Daruma in public — you had to climb up somewhere high to see it whole. This was not whimsy: he was promoting the Hokusai Manga in the region, and it was a precisely timed piece of marketing. He went from "an artist who takes commissions" to "a name that brings its own audience," and from then on the name itself was the product.

Second: the fire of 1839. The studio burned, and decades of accumulated drawings and sketchbooks went with it. He was seventy-nine and had just been drained by his grandson's gambling debts — a man whose work ran on a parts library, with the parts library gone. He made no more systematic print series after that, turning instead to painting one brush painting at a time, including Dragon Rising Above Mount Fuji from the first month of the year he died. The fire forced him back to one man and one sheet of paper, and the work of his last decade is generally judged his loosest and least product-like.

Source: Iijima Kyoshin, Katsushika Hokusai den (1893).

◆Character & Habits: a lion a day thrown away, moving house instead of cleaning, money left in a corner

From the age of eighty-three, a lion every morning, discarded when finished. From 1842 he made drawing a Chinese lion at dawn a daily exorcism (the Nisshin joma sheets), throwing each away; the two hundred-odd that survive were picked up by other people. The point isn't diligence — it's that he kept one channel of daily output that was destroyed the same day and never priced by the market.

He didn't clean; when it got filthy he moved — ninety-three times in a life. By his pupil Tsuyuki Iitsu's recollection, he worked year-round wrapped in a kotatsu, and when paper scraps and food remains made the floor unusable he simply took another lodging.

He didn't handle money. Payment arrived and he wouldn't even open the wrapper — he tossed it in a corner, and callers collecting debts helped themselves to what they were owed from the pile. He made tens of thousands of pictures and was destitute twice in old age.

He used names as phase markers and gave them away when finished. At sixty he became "Iitsu" — back to one — and passed the old name "Taito" to a pupil; at seventy-four he signed "Gakyō Rōjin Manji," the old man mad about painting. Renaming wasn't an affectation; it was a public announcement that the previous version of him was over. After a stroke in 1827 he mixed his own citron-and-shochu remedy and dosed himself with it.

Source: Iijima Kyoshin, Katsushika Hokusai den (1893), including Tsuyuki Iitsu's recollections.

◆Controversy & Shadow: the erotica edited out, the household drained, the forty years buried under a self-myth

First: erotic books were a significant part of his income and his output, and today's narrative cuts them out. "The Dream of the Fisherman's Wife" from the 1814 erotic book Kinoe no Komatsu — a diver and two octopuses — is among the most famous and most contested images in the history of ukiyo-e. The dispute isn't only about explicitness: it aestheticizes a scene between a non-human creature and a woman's body, and has been quoted and imitated so persistently since that it founded a visual lineage still running today.

Second: his late productivity rested on one daughter's full-time support. His money was repeatedly drained by his grandson's gambling debts, and in 1834 he fled to Uraga under an assumed name to escape creditors. In the same period his daughter Katsushika Ōi left her husband and came back to live with him, absorbing the household and the running of the studio — and she was a superb painter in her own right, the night light of her Yoshiwara at Night still an anomaly in ukiyo-e. Scholars widely suspect that a substantial share of the coloring in "late Hokusai" is her hand, and only about ten works survive that are firmly hers. He could give all his time to painting because someone else carried all the rest of it.

Third: "nothing before seventy is worth anything" is both a masterpiece of a line and a highly effective piece of self-marketing. That postscript wrote off forty years of his own commercial work at a stroke, set the key for the "old man mad about painting," and set how posterity would read him. He also passed used names on to pupils to inherit (a second Taito, for instance), so work of uneven quality circulated under the Katsushika brand — a problem for attribution to this day. He announced that he was resetting to zero, and sold off the name he had zeroed out.

Sources: Timothy Clark, Hokusai: Beyond the Great Wave (2017); Ota Memorial Museum of Art exhibition catalogue on Katsushika Ōi.

◆What BigCat Can Take From This

The most transferable thing is putting the reset on the calendar instead of waiting for a crisis to force it: the renamings, the handed-off names, the daily lion thrown away the same day — all one mechanism, a deliberately maintained channel of output that isn't priced against past results. The failure mode for a technical person isn't being unable to learn; it's being locked in by the very stack that has become valuable. Second, the Hokusai Manga shows that distilling a private method into a publicly usable parts library gives more leverage than guarding it. Third, the Prussian blue bet at seventy: the best adopter of a new tool is often not the youngest person but the veteran who already has a mature system of expression and is short exactly one new variable. But read the invoice too: he could go all-in because Katsushika Ōi carried everything else. "All my time on the core work" is not a personal virtue; it's a bill someone else pays.

◆Going Deeper

If Hokusai were born today, who would he be?
His core capability wasn't brushwork; it was decomposing the world into reusable graphic parts and recombining them without limit — the Hokusai Manga is essentially an open asset library plus a style manual. The nearest position today may not be in art at all, but in building tools, asset systems, or methodology other people can call. Two things would differ: that parts library is exactly what generative models learn most easily, and the clean break from his own past that renaming gave him is nearly impossible now.
Which of his conditions can't be reproduced?
At least three: an exceptionally long healthy life (surviving to eighty-eight in the Edo period was itself unlikely, and all his defining work came after seventy); a mature print industry — the division of labor between designer, carver, printer and publisher let one man's drawing be mass-copied and sold cheap, an amplifier he didn't build; and one family member working full time in self-erasure. Only the method transfers: a daily disposable output, deliberately changing your technical stack, and opening private methods into public assets.
What do his blind spots reveal?
Toward everything outside the picture — money, housing, his family's time — his policy was "don't deal with it." That isn't just eccentricity; it's a default setting that outsources complexity. The question worth asking isn't "am I focused enough," it's "the whole class of things I don't deal with in order to stay focused — whose account is that charged to right now?" The other half is the power of self-narrative: one line about nothing before seventy being worth anything cost posterity forty years of his work. How you define yourself decides which part of you other people look at.
