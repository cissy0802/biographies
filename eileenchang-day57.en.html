Biography: Eileen Chang
Zhang Ailing

1920 — 1995 · Died at 74
Novelist · Essayist · Translator · Romances, The Golden Cangue, Little Reunions
What is worth studying is not the "gifted woman." Very early she saw through to the undignified half of human nature and built an entire aesthetic on that clarity; but the same clarity let her file politics under private life and make retreat her default move — enormous insight, almost never pointed at action.

◆Life in Brief

Born 30 September 1920 in Shanghai into a great family already collapsing: her grandfather Zhang Peilun was a late-Qing censor, her grandmother a daughter of Li Hongzhang; by her father Zhang Zhiyi's generation what remained was opium and the manners of a stranded aristocracy, while her mother Huang Yifan studied abroad, divorced, and was mostly absent. In spring 1943, aged 23, she carried a manuscript to the editor Zhou Shoujuan; within two years she had written Love in a Fallen City, The Golden Cangue and Sealed Off, and was the most-read writer in occupied Shanghai. In 1944 she married Hu Lancheng, an official of the collaborationist Wang Jingwei regime, and after the war fell into silence with him. She left Shanghai for Hong Kong in 1952 and the United States in 1955, living the next forty years on translation and scattered fees. She died alone in a Los Angeles apartment in September 1995.

◆A Curve That Kept Retreating

  • 1937—1938A clash with her stepmother; her father beats her and confines her for nearly six months. She escapes by night in early 1938 to her mother.
  • 1939—1942Admitted to the University of London; war sends her to Hong Kong University instead; returns to Shanghai after the fighting.
  • May 1943"Aloeswood Incense: The First Brazier" appears; within a year she is famous across Shanghai.
  • Aug 1944Romances sells out in four days; she marries Hu Lancheng the same year.
  • 1945—1946Japan's defeat; she is named in the "cultural traitor" attacks and has almost nowhere left to publish.
  • Jul 1952Leaves Shanghai on the pretext of resuming her degree in Hong Kong; never returns.
  • 1954—1955Writes The Rice-Sprout Song and Naked Earth under US Information Service sponsorship, then sails for America.
  • Aug 1956Marries the playwright Ferdinand Reyher, 36 years her senior; strokes soon follow.
  • 1967—1977Reyher dies; a Berkeley research post ends in dismissal for low output; ten years of textual work yield Nightmare in the Red Chamber.
  • 1984—1988Convinced of an infestation, she moves between motels more than a hundred times in three years.
  • Sep 1995Dies; her will asks for cremation, ashes scattered in open country, and no ceremony.

◆Key Decisions: the escape, the wrong magazine, the 1952 exit permit

One — the night escape of early 1938. Over a single remark from her stepmother, her father struck her in front of others and then shut her in an empty room for nearly six months; when she came down with dysentery he refused to call a doctor. What she recorded afterwards was not fear but arithmetic: she timed the patrolmen's shift change and walked out through the gap. Her mother then handed her a choice — marry or study, there was money for only one — and she chose to study. This is the template for every later decision she made: no argument, no confrontation, just a calculated route and then disappearance.

Two — spring 1943, sending her manuscript to a popular magazine rather than the serious literary camp. The passport into respectable letters was enlightenment and national salvation; she went straight to Zhou Shoujuan, an old editor of the "mandarin ducks and butterflies" school, and inside two years had a market and a name. The bill came due two years later: the stage she rose on was occupied Shanghai, and after the victory the stage and everyone standing on it were cleared away together.

Three — July 1952, applying to leave "to resume her studies in Hong Kong." In 1950 she had attended Shanghai's first literary congress in a qipao, conspicuous in a hall of Lenin suits; she could already see that her subject — the calculations and self-deceptions of ordinary city people — had no place in the new literary demands. She left before anyone asked her to reform: the timing was exact, and the price was forty years with no first-hand Chinese material left to write.

Sources: Eileen Chang, "Whispers" (1944) and Mutual Reflections (1994); Zhang Ailing's Private Words, ed. Roland Soong (2010).

◆Turning Points: the 1943 eruption, the 1945 cliff

First: from unknown to famous across Shanghai in under a year. In spring 1943 an introduction took her to Zhou Shoujuan, and she left the "Aloeswood Incense" manuscript with him. He read it through that night and wrote a special preface when it ran, saying it was like reading Dream of the Red Chamber and Somerset Maugham at once. In the year and a bit after that May debut came Love in a Fallen City, The Golden Cangue, Sealed Off — nearly the whole of her reputation over the next fifty years rests on those under-two years.

Second: after August 1945 the stage was removed entirely. For publishing in occupied-zone magazines and for her marriage to a propaganda official of the collaborationist regime, she was publicly named in the "cultural traitor" attacks; never formally charged, she nonetheless had almost nowhere to publish for two or three years. What matters is her response: no defence, no statement, no self-criticism — just a quiet change of trade. From 1947 she wrote screenplays for Wenhua Studio, Love Without End and Long Live the Mistress!, and went on earning under another identity. When an environment turned hostile she never repaired the relationship; she only switched tracks. It let her survive again and again — and start from zero again and again.

Sources: Zhou Shoujuan, "Written Before Violet" (1943); C. T. Hsia, A History of Modern Chinese Fiction (1961).

◆Character and Habits: cutting her own clothes, revising for twenty years, a hundred moves in three

Clothes as a wearable position. She designed her own, once making an overcoat from the fabric of her grandmother's dowry quilt and wearing it to the printing house, drawing stares in 1940s Shanghai. Dress, she wrote, is "a pocket-sized drama carried on the person" — in an age that asked people to dress like a collective, she insisted on dressing like a person.

The same story, rewritten for twenty years. Eighteen Springs became Half a Lifelong Romance; The Golden Cangue she first translated into English herself, then rewrote back into Chinese as The Rouge of the North; The Sing-song Girls of Shanghai she translated twice, starting over when the English draft was lost. Her low output was not laziness but the fact that she did almost one thing only: write again what she had already written.

Seeing no one, as a system. Through her last twenty-odd years in Los Angeles she refused nearly every visitor, keeping contact only by letter with the Soongs, C. T. Hsia and Chuang Hsin-cheng.

Between 1984 and 1988, certain her rooms held fleas, she moved between motels constantly — by her own letters more than a hundred times. To stay mobile she threw out furniture, clothing, even parts of her manuscripts. Her executor Lin Shitong recorded that her final rooms held almost no furniture: a camp bed, paper plates and cups. This was not only illness. That posture — able to drop everything and walk out at any moment — had not changed since the night in 1938 she escaped her father's house.

Sources: Eileen Chang, "A Chronicle of Changing Clothes" (1943); Lin Shitong, "Knowing Eileen Chang" (1996).

◆Controversy and Shadow: treason as a private matter, a commissioned novel, the closed door to family

One — Hu Lancheng. When they signed their marriage document in 1944 he was editor-in-chief of the collaborationist regime's China Daily News and a senior figure in its propaganda apparatus — not a difference of opinion but a public role no one could mistake. She treated the relationship as purely private and never once discussed its political meaning in public; when she wrote to break it off in June 1947, her reason was that he had another woman in Wenzhou. The stated grounds were betrayal of her feelings, not betrayal of a country.

Two — Naked Earth. In 1954–55 she wrote in Hong Kong on United States Information Service money. The Rice-Sprout Song was her own conception, but the outline of Naked Earth was supplied to her; she later said flatly in letters that it was not what she wanted to write and was "not well done." A writer who refused all her life to write for any camp took a commission once, for the fee, and produced the least defensible book in her body of work — one both Beijing and Taipei then used as propaganda and as a target.

Three — the door closed on her own family. In 1957 her mother Huang Yifan, dying in London, wrote asking to see her one last time; she did not go, sent a hundred dollars, and afterwards lived by selling the antiques her mother had left. Her brother Zhang Zijing came to her in 1946 hoping to live with her and was refused; in old age he lived poor and alone in Shanghai, the two of them almost entirely out of contact. The cold eye that fuelled her fiction — seeing through the calculation and self-deception inside every family bond — also cut away her own last line of retreat. No character of hers is ever saved by tenderness; neither was she.

Sources: Hu Lancheng, This Life, These Times (1959); letters in Zhang Ailing's Private Words (2010); Zhang Zijing, My Elder Sister Eileen Chang (1996).

◆What BigCat Can Take From This

The first thing worth taking is "narrow and deep": she wrote about one thing all her life — how ordinary people calculate their own interest in a collapsing world and lie to themselves about it — and wrote it so well that no one could replace her. In an era where tools flatten capability across the board, a defensible position comes not from coverage but from one corner nobody else is willing to stare at for years. The second is exit discipline: in 1952 she judged that her subject had no home in the new environment, so she left early, without pleading her case and without remaking herself to fit — leaving early is judgement; leaving late is being thrown out. The third is the negative one: her insight almost never pointed at action. She saw through her father, her mother, her husband and her age, yet repaired no relationship and built no support system for herself. Seeing clearly and living well are two different things — insight that is not wired into relationships and collaboration only shrinks a person, step by step, into a smaller and smaller room.

◆Going Deeper

If Eileen Chang were born today, what would happen to her?
The barrier to publishing is gone — she would need no Zhou Shoujuan; those two braziers of incense could go out under her own name, and her extreme avoidance of people would even be an advantage in a remote-work age. The real question is whether she could still burn bright for two years and then stop. Continuous exposure demands constant output, replies, a maintained persona — and her whole force came from refusing to explain and refusing to take part. She might become famous sooner, and be consumed sooner into an account repeating itself.
Were the conditions of her success reproducible?
The raw material was not: a great family in the act of dissolving, a father who locked her up, a city sealed off by war — her best work is quarried entirely from that rubble. Only two things transfer: put all your attention on a subject others consider beneath them, and look long enough before you write. One thing transfers in reverse, too: withdrawal, cutting contact, treating every relationship as disposable. That costs almost nothing to copy, and the price is the same as hers.
What does her blind spot reveal?
She demoted everything public into something private: marrying an official of a collaborationist regime was, to her, marrying a man who understood her; being named on a list of traitors was bad luck. The demotion preserved her sensibility, and left her completely unable to feel what she had stepped into. Anyone who defines themselves as "just doing my own work" faces the same problem: who you collaborate with, where the funding comes from, which platform you publish on — those are positions in themselves, and they do not become private matters because you would rather not discuss them.
