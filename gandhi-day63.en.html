Biography: Mahatma Gandhi

1869 — 1948 · aged 78
lawyer · organiser of the Indian diaspora in South Africa · leader of the independence movement · inventor of satyagraha
What is worth learning is not the title "Mahatma." He turned a moral position into an executable piece of action design: pick a move anyone can copy, that costs almost nothing, and that looks absurd to suppress. And the warning: the same ethic puts self-purification at the head of the causal chain, so the bill for insufficient purity is always paid first by the people closest to him.

◆Life in Brief

Born in 1869 in Porbandar, Gujarat, the son of the chief minister of a princely state. He read law in London from 1888 and was called to the bar in 1891, then froze in silence at his first court appearance back in India. In 1893 he took a one-year contract in South Africa and stayed twenty-one — it was there that he ground diaspora protest into a reproducible method. He returned to India in 1915, spent a year touring under a vow to observe and not speak, and won his first domestic case with the indigo farmers of Bihar in 1917. Three nationwide movements followed. Independence and partition arrived together in 1947; a year later he was shot dead by a Hindu nationalist.

◆Key Decisions: rename the method, brake at full speed, pick up a pinch of salt

1. 11 September 1906: swear first, then rename. The Transvaal required every Asian resident to register fingerprints — the "Black Act," as the community called it. That evening about three thousand people packed the Empire Theatre in Johannesburg, and what Gandhi proposed was not a petition but an oath before God, taken on the spot, to refuse registration — turning a vote into an individual promise that cannot be withdrawn. He then offered a prize for a new name, pulling the method out of the ready-made English term "passive resistance" and calling it satyagraha, holding firmly to truth — because passive implies the last resort of the weak. Naming is not rhetoric; it is severance.

2. February 1922: brake at maximum momentum. Non-cooperation was sweeping the country when, on 4 February, demonstrators in Chauri Chaura in the United Provinces burned down a police station, killing more than twenty policemen. Gandhi halted the national movement immediately and fasted for five days. Nehru, in prison, recorded his bafflement: why should one remote town's loss of control zero out a nationwide mobilisation? Gandhi's logic: purity of means is not decoration, it is the sole source of power this method has — once a crowd has learned arson, nonviolence is merely violence not yet cashed in.

3. 1930: why salt. The hard part was not deciding to defy the law, it was choosing the target. Nehru and the Congress Working Committee initially thought salt too trivial, not a constitutional issue. Gandhi's calculation: the salt tax forced the poorest to pay for something the sea deposits by itself, making illegal salt cheap enough for anyone to copy without organisation — and arresting an old man for scooping brine off a beach looks absurd to any audience. On 12 March he set out with seventy-eight followers, walked twenty-four days and some 385 kilometres, and at dawn on 6 April picked up a pinch of salt at Dandi. The finish came at the Dharasana salt works on 21 May: line after line of marchers walked up, were clubbed down, and neither struck back nor flinched, while the American reporter Webb Miller's dispatch ran in hundreds of newspapers. What he built was not an action but a machine that converts repression into the other side's loss of legitimacy.

Sources: M. K. Gandhi, An Autobiography: The Story of My Experiments with Truth (1927–29), Part IV; Young India, 16 Feb 1922, "The Crime of Chauri Chaura"; Judith M. Brown, Gandhi: Prisoner of Hope (1989), Ch. 5, 7; Webb Miller, I Found No Peace (1936).

◆The Turn: that railway carriage in 1893, and the half-sentence the myth leaves out

One night in June 1893 the twenty-three-year-old Gandhi was travelling from Durban to Pretoria on a first-class ticket. After a white passenger complained, the conductor ordered him to the luggage van; he refused, his baggage was thrown onto the platform and he was pushed off the train. He sat through the freezing night in the Pietermaritzburg waiting room weighing whether to buy a ticket home and be a safe lawyer in India, or stay. He later called it the most creative experience of his life.

The myth usually stops here, and the second half is the more instructive part. What he was arguing for was not that all people are equal, but that educated Indians should not be treated as "kaffirs" (a slur for Black Africans) — moving Indians one rung up the racial ladder, not dismantling it. Awakening usually begins with "people like me have been wronged" rather than "this ranking has no basis"; what matters is whether you keep walking afterwards. Gandhi did — very late.

Sources: Gandhi, An Autobiography, Part II, Ch. 8; Ramachandra Guha, Gandhi Before India (2013), Ch. 4; Ashwin Desai & Goolam Vahed, The South African Gandhi (2015).

◆Character & Habits: silent Mondays, the daily spindle, cleaning the latrines himself

A full day of silence every Monday. From the mid-1920s he kept Mondays as a day of silence (maun vaar), holding to it through negotiations, visitors and political crises alike — if you needed to talk, he wrote notes. He converted "not responding" from an attitude into a calendar constraint, so it no longer required willpower.

Spinning every day. He sat at the wheel for a fixed period daily until the year he died; after 1924 Congress for a time counted hand-spun yarn as part of membership dues. Hand yarn was the economic counterpart of boycotting British mill cloth, and a task you had to perform with your hands each day turned an abstract boycott into a daily exercise every member ran.

Cleaning the latrines himself. At Tolstoy Farm and the Sabarmati ashram he required every member to take a turn removing night soil — in a caste society, work assigned to "untouchables." He did it first, and required his wife Kasturba to do it too. They fought bitterly over this in Durban in 1898, and he recorded it honestly in his autobiography: he had dragged his weeping wife towards the gate to put her out of the house. The first subject of his moral experiments was always his own family.

Sources: Gandhi, An Autobiography, Part I, Ch. 22 & Part IV; Louis Fischer, The Life of Mahatma Gandhi (1950), Ch. 12, 19; The Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi (100 vols).

◆Controversy & Shadow: fasting as coercion, people as purity instruments, the family's bill

1. 1932: staking his own life as the bargaining chip. Britain's Communal Award gave Dalits ("untouchables") separate electorates, which Ambedkar saw as the first political seats the oppressed would hold free of upper-caste control. On 20 September, Gandhi began a fast unto death in prison against it. The pressure landed instantly on Ambedkar: if he did not yield and Gandhi died, Dalit communities would face reprisal violence. Days later they signed the Poona Pact, trading separate electorates for reserved seats. Ambedkar later wrote that he had been forced to choose between Gandhi's life and his own people's political rights. Nonviolence applied to yourself is still coercion of the other party — it just books no violence on the ledger.

2. The brahmacharya experiments of 1946–47. During the communal killings in Noakhali, Gandhi, then nearly seventy-seven, had his grand-niece Manu and other young women sleep naked beside him to test the purity of his celibacy; the inference was that violence outside meant he was not yet pure enough. Two long-serving aides resigned over it. The problem is not celibacy but that this causal scheme licensed him to conscript other people's bodies as instruments of his own practice, and they were in no position to refuse.

3. The family's bill. His eldest son Harilal wanted to study law in England; Gandhi refused on the grounds that he should not hand such privilege to his own son. Father and son broke apart, Harilal drank, converted more than once, and died of illness months after Gandhi's assassination. In February 1944, with Kasturba dying in detention, doctors advised penicillin injections; Gandhi refused, saying he did not want her final hours spent under the needle. Weeks later he took quinine for his own malaria. This need not be hypocrisy, but it shows what happens when someone who ranks purity above outcomes decides for another person: the cost is not his to bear.

Sources: B. R. Ambedkar, What Congress and Gandhi Have Done to the Untouchables (1945); Judith M. Brown, Gandhi: Prisoner of Hope (1989), Ch. 9; Rajmohan Gandhi, Mohandas (2006), Part V.

◆What BigCat Can Take Away

The most transferable thing is his algorithm for choosing a target. Do not pick the "most important" problem; pick an action with the lowest threshold, executable by any individual alone, that makes the opponent look absurd for suppressing it — salt moved an empire not because the salt tax mattered but because it satisfied all three at once. In distributed-systems terms: find the smallest primitive that everyone can run, that needs no coordination, and whose failure does not damage the body, then let scale grow on its own. Second: anything a single local failure can destroy should not have been scaled up first.

The third one stings. His ethic places self-purification at the head of the causal chain, so any external failure can be explained as insufficient purity — an unfalsifiable framework never stops on its own; it keeps raising its demands, and the cost is paid first by whoever is closest to you. For anyone chasing the "AI super-individual," the trap has almost the same shape: attribute every outcome to the intensity of your own practice, and it is your family who settles the account for that story.

◆Going Deeper

If Gandhi were born today, could he still operate this way?
The fuse of the Salt March was a very narrow information channel: one dispatch, carried by hundreds of newspapers to the voters of a metropole that cared about its own civilising story. Today images of repression travel in seconds, but so do denial and noise, and the same photograph is digested by three competing frames at once. Tempo is a problem too: a twenty-four-day walk was a serially escalating story then and would not survive day four now. The design principle still transfers; the new problem is resisting dilution.
Are the conditions of his success reproducible?
Mostly not. His opponent was an empire that had to sustain a narrative of civilised rule, that had a parliament and a free press as internal echo chambers, and that was financially broken after the Second World War. The leverage point of nonviolence is precisely the other side's concern for its own legitimacy — the less they care, the shorter the lever. His blind spot proves it: in November 1938 he wrote in Harijan advising German Jews to meet the Nazis with nonviolence. A method has a domain of validity; treating it as a universal law is a misuse.
What do his blind spots reveal?
His framework has a fixed shape: every external problem is first translated into a problem of inner purity. The benefit is that it always gives you an executable next step; the cost is that it is unfalsifiable and its expenses spill onto the people around you. It also left him insensitive to structural power — on caste, his remedy was moral self-renewal by the upper castes plus a name he coined, "Harijan" (children of God), while Ambedkar wanted votes and statutes. A person's sharpest tool rewrites every problem into the kind it can handle; someone skilled at reforming himself instinctively believes the problem lies in hearts rather than institutions.
