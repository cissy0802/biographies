Biography: Yayoi Kusama

1929 — present
Avant-garde artist · polka dots and infinity mirror rooms · a world-class painter who checked herself into a psychiatric hospital
Hallucinations have besieged her since she was ten, and she has never been cured. What she built was not a victory over illness but a production line for it: her hospital room and her studio sit on opposite sides of one street. She has clocked in and out, and painted, for seventy years.

◆Life in Brief

Born in 1929 in Matsumoto, Nagano Prefecture, into a wealthy, oppressive family of seed merchants. From around the age of ten she experienced visual and auditory hallucinations — patterns creeping off the tablecloth to cover the room and her own body. Her mother destroyed her drawings and sent her to spy on her father's affairs. In 1957 she left for America, settling in New York the next year, and broke into the avant-garde with vast Infinity Nets paintings, the Accumulation series of soft phallic sculptures, and mirrored infinity spaces. By the late 1960s her nude anti-Vietnam-War "happenings" had made her notorious. In 1973 illness drove her back to Japan, where she was all but forgotten and lived for a time by writing novels. In 1977 she voluntarily admitted herself to a Tokyo psychiatric hospital and opened a studio across the street; she has made that commute daily for nearly fifty years. In 1993 she represented Japan with a solo pavilion at the Venice Biennale — her comeback. Infinity mirror rooms then swept the world's museums, and she became the highest-selling living woman artist at auction.

◆Key Decisions: A Letter to a Stranger, Burning Her Own Work, Turning a Hospital into a Base

First, on 15 November 1955, she wrote to Georgia O'Keeffe, whom she had never met. She had found a book of O'Keeffe's paintings in a secondhand shop in Matsumoto, had someone track down an address, and mailed a few watercolours to New Mexico with a letter asking for help. On 4 December O'Keeffe replied by hand — bluntly telling her how hard this path was, but that if she truly meant to take it, she had to come to New York. A provincial Japanese girl pried open an entire life trajectory with a letter that had almost no chance of being answered.

Second, in 1957, before leaving the country, she burned thousands of her early works on a riverbank. This was not performance art but the cutting of a retreat: her family opposed the move, and she left nothing behind she could crawl back to. She took only a few paintings and cash sewn into her clothing (currency controls were severe at the time).

Third, in 1977 she admitted herself to a psychiatric hospital and rented a studio directly across the road. She was not committed; she walked in. Nights under care at the hospital, days painting across the street — an uncontrollable illness packed into a controllable schedule. Most people treat illness as an obstacle to be removed; she treated it as a permanent collaborator, and so began by designing the physical layout of the relationship.

Sources: Kusama's autobiography Infinity Net (2002 Japanese edition / 2011 English translation); Tate and Artnet reporting on the O'Keeffe correspondence.

◆Turning Point: From Chased Off the Lawn at Venice to Representing Japan on the Same Stage

In 1966, having received no invitation to the Venice Biennale, she paid her own way and laid 1,500 plastic mirror balls on the lawn outside the Italian pavilion. She called it Narcissus Garden and sold the spheres on the spot for 1,200 lire each (about $2), beside a sign reading "Your Narcissism for Sale." Organisers stopped her, on the grounds that art could not be hawked like hot dogs. An Asian woman who wanted in could only stand outside the door, selling her own existence back as satire.

The deeper trough came later. Returning to Japan in 1973 after a physical and mental collapse, she found that her New York avant-garde record read as scandal to the Japanese press of the day; galleries would not touch her, and she survived by writing novels. Not until 1993 was she chosen as the solo artist for Japan's national pavilion, returning to Venice as the protagonist — twenty-seven years after being chased off that lawn. Same venue; two decades of near-total obscurity in between.

Sources: Infinity Net; Tate Modern 2012 retrospective catalogue.

◆Character and Habits: A Timetable That Turns Hallucination into Output

Nearly fifty years of the same commute: a few minutes' walk from her hospital room to the studio opposite, painting until evening, then walking back. Into her nineties she has kept the rhythm from a wheelchair, with assistants handing her brushes and paint.

Repetitive labour with extraordinary stamina. The Infinity Net shown at her 1959 New York debut was some ten metres wide, packed with well over a million hand-painted arcs; she has described painting for more than forty hours without sleep, until her arm went numb and hallucination and canvas merged.

Fear used directly as material. The spying her mother forced on her left her with an extreme fear of sex — so she sewed thousands of phallic fabric protrusions and covered armchairs, shoes and rowboats with them. She called it psychosomatic art: not avoiding the fear, but replicating it until it loses its power to threaten.

A ferocious instinct for self-promotion. In New York she mailed photographs of her work and press releases to newspapers unprompted, tipped off the media before every happening, and in 1968 published an open letter to Nixon offering her body in exchange for ending the Vietnam War — protest, and a precise operation on attention.

Sources: Infinity Net; Tate 2012 retrospective catalogue; the documentary Kusama: Infinity (2018).

◆Controversy and Shadow: Priority Claims, Racist Early Writing, Suffering Turned into Merchandise

First, the recurring "they copied me" accusations. She has publicly claimed that Oldenburg's soft sculpture, Warhol's wall-to-wall repeated images and Samaras's mirror room all derived from her earlier work, and linked her 1966 suicide attempt to it. She was demonstrably first in several places on the timeline, but the accusations cannot be verified — and the image of "the buried pioneer" has been cultivated for decades by her autobiography and studio, with the ambiguous parts trimmed in her favour.

Second, the racist passages surfaced in October 2023. On the eve of her major show at the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art, the press reported demeaning depictions of Black people in her early writing: a 1971 play casting its only Black character as a savage, a 1984 novel objectifying Black bodies, and a passage in the Japanese edition of her autobiography blaming Black residents for turning Greenwich Village into a slum (cut from the English translation). She then expressed "deep regret" through the San Francisco Chronicle. Someone discriminated against all her life as a woman, an Asian and a psychiatric patient was also writing about others in the language of discrimination.

Third, pain packaged as a photo opportunity. Two global Louis Vuitton collaborations, in 2012 and 2023, plastered dots across windows and handbags; mirror rooms admit visitors for thirty seconds and have become selfie backdrops, while the work is produced by a large team. Critics argue that something born of hallucination and self-obliteration has been compressed into a single social-media image — what the audience consumes is spectacle, not the fear.

Sources: ARTnews and Artnet reporting, October 2023; San Francisco Chronicle, October 2023; Tate 2012 retrospective catalogue.

◆Takeaways for BigCat

What she offers is not an inspirational story but an engineering solution for collaborating long-term with something that cannot be fixed. She does not wait to feel well before working, and does not expect a cure; she redefines her most unstable variables — hallucination, anxiety — from "faults to be eliminated" into "raw material," then builds rigid structure around them: a fixed route, fixed hours, non-core steps outsourced, only the irreplaceable layer kept for herself. For anyone chasing the "super-individual," the real leverage rarely comes from removing your own instability; it comes from designing a system that can hold it and still ship, day after day. Second, the compounding of a single motif: seventy years doing one thing — covering every surface with a repeating unit — from canvas to mirror room to handbag. The medium changed seven or eight times; the formal language never did. Third, a warning: her control over her own narrative is so seamless that it doubles as a moat, which is also why the stains in her old writing went unexamined for decades. People who tell their own story too tightly have usually stopped being corrected.

◆Going Deeper

If Kusama were born today, would social platforms make her or consume her?
Her work is built for transmission: instantly recognisable, enterable, photographable — and she was already mailing press releases to the media in the 1960s, which is the same logic. Algorithms would only get her seen faster. But the cost is sharper: a mirror room is a spatial experience about dissolving the self, and compressed into one photograph it delivers spectacle rather than the sensation of being swallowed. Tools amplify the most transmissible side of you, which is not necessarily the side you most want understood.
Is the hospital-and-studio structure a transferable method, or her special case?
The structure is copyable; the price may not be one you would pay. She compressed life down to almost nothing but work — no family, no social life. What transfers is the principle: identify the constraint that will not go away, stop negotiating with it, and rearrange the environment around it — shorten the distance from waking to workstation, fix the hours, outsource the non-core steps. Ask yourself: if I accept that a given problem will never get better, what would my schedule look like? Most people are stuck precisely because they are still waiting for it to improve first.
How should her racist early writing and her own experience of persecution be held together?
Cancelling one against the other is the lazy move: suffering does not absolve those passages, and those passages do not erase her achievement. The more useful reading is that the experience of being oppressed grants no automatic moral exemption — a person can be exquisitely sensitive along the axis where they were wounded and entirely numb along another. The bias we overlook most easily tends to sit exactly where we have never been hurt.
