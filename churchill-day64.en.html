Biography: Winston Churchill

1874 — 1965 · died at 90
journalist · First Lord of the Admiralty · Chancellor of the Exchequer · twice Prime Minister · Nobel Prize in Literature, 1953
What is worth learning: in May 1940, he saw that the real fork was not "fight or surrender" but "do we start asking about terms at all". What is worth fearing: the certainty that made him right in 1940 was the same engine — it also drove him, and others, into the walls of 1915, 1925 and 1943.

◆Life in Brief

Born in 1874 at Blenheim Palace, a junior branch of a ducal family; his father died young, his mother was American. He made his name in his twenties as a war correspondent, won a seat in the Commons on returning home, and for the next half-century was rarely far from power: Admiralty, Exchequer, a decade shut out by the establishment, two terms as Prime Minister. His trajectory is not a rising line but a series of violent oscillations (below). In 1953 he won the Nobel Prize in Literature for his memoirs and oratory.

◆Key Decision — May 1940: what he refused was not surrender, but the asking

Between 26 and 28 May the War Cabinet met nine times. The French line was broken, the British Expeditionary Force was pinned on the beaches at Dunkirk (7,669 men came off on the first day), and America was neutral. What Foreign Secretary Halifax proposed was not surrender but something that sounded mild: use Mussolini, still out of the war, to sound out what terms Germany might offer — just listen, commit to nothing.

Churchill's answer was not a posture but a judgement about slippage: once you start asking, word gets out, and the will to resist collapses before the army does; and your leverage only decays — terms unacceptable today will look generous in three months. At four o'clock on the 28th he called the twenty-five ministers outside the War Cabinet, men normally excluded from the highest decisions, into his own room, and spoke briefly (the closing line is quoted below). The room rose and shouted. He carried that noise back into the War Cabinet, and the proposal was never raised again. The lesson is in locating the decision, not in courage: the real fork is often not the largest option on the table but a small, apparently harmless move that changes every option after it.

Five weeks later came a decision of the opposite character: on 3 July the Royal Navy opened fire on the French fleet — a former ally — at Mers-el-Kébir in Algeria, killing 1,297 men. It was a signal too expensive to fake, addressed to Washington: this government will fight, and can bring itself to do the ugly thing.

Sources: John Lukacs, Five Days in London, May 1940 (1999); Hugh Dalton's diary, 28 May 1940; Martin Gilbert, Winston S. Churchill, Vol. VI: Finest Hour.

◆The Pivot — the 1915 resignation, and the eight years that actually mattered

On 15 November 1915 he made his resignation speech to the Commons. Gallipoli — the Dardanelles scheme he had pushed, meant to break the deadlock in the West — had cost the Allies some 250,000 casualties. He did not stay in London waiting to be recalled; he asked for a front-line command, and from January 1916 spent four months in Belgium as a battalion commander in the 6th Royal Scots Fusiliers. It gave him a credential — I have stood in that trench. But the pivot actually failed: Gallipoli never really lowered his estimate of his own judgement, and in 1925 and 1943 he would make the same class of error — overconfidence in an elegant scheme, and a systematic underrating of the logistics and the people in the execution chain.

What changed his position was 1931—1939. He lost most of his savings in the New York crash of October 1929 and lived off his writing thereafter; by 1938 Chartwell was on the market, saved only when the financier Henry Strakosch quietly took over some £18,000 of his debts. For eight years he spoke from the back benches about German rearmament and was treated by his own party as a spent troublemaker. By 1940 that isolation had become the qualification: he was the man with a documented record of having been right.

Sources: Martin Gilbert, Churchill: A Life (1991); Roy Jenkins, Churchill (2001), on the wilderness years and his finances.

◆Character & Habits — two days in one, dictate everything, treat speech as engineering

Two working days inside one. Awake at 8, he worked three hours in bed — newspapers, dictation, secretaries — and did not get up and bathe until 11. After lunch he undressed and slept one to two hours, bathed a second time in the evening, and worked from 10 p.m. until two or three in the morning. He said it let him fit two working days into one; the cost fell on his staff, and Number 10 had to run a night shift.

He almost never wrote by hand. Memoranda, speeches, books — nearly all of it was dictated to a stenographer while he paced. Drafts were set out in "psalm form", broken and indented by breath and stress: on the platform he was reading rhythm, not sentences. Nor was the oratory a gift. He had a speech impediment on his s-sounds, and on 22 April 1904 he lost his thread mid-speech in the Commons, stood frozen for about three minutes, and sat down. After that he almost never improvised — every line that sounds spontaneous was written and memorised in advance.

"ACTION THIS DAY" and the one-page rule. On 9 August 1940 he issued a memo titled Brevity: submissions were to be compressed to a single sheet, with complex matters relegated to appendices — the time saved in not reading padding can only be bought by not writing it. Genuinely urgent files carried a red ACTION THIS DAY tag. It is attention routing designed at prime-ministerial scale.

Use a different set of muscles. After Gallipoli he took up oil painting — some 550 canvases — and in 1928 laid several hundred metres of brick wall at Chartwell himself. A tired mind, he argued, cannot be restored by switching off the lights; another area has to be lit.

Sources: Winston Churchill, Painting as a Pastime (1948); Elizabeth Nel, Mr Churchill's Secretary (1958); the Brevity memorandum, 9 August 1940.

◆Controversy & Shadow — Bengal, the gold standard, and an imperial view he never hid

The Bengal famine of 1943 (estimates of 2.1 to 3 million dead). The causes were compound: the Japanese occupation of Burma cut off Burmese rice, then a cyclone, hoarding, and a failing provincial administration. But London's share of the responsibility sits in a string of specific refusals — Viceroy Wavell asked repeatedly for grain ships, and the War Cabinet gave shipping priority to the Mediterranean theatre and to post-war European stockpiles. Leo Amery, Secretary of State for India, recorded in his diary in September 1944 Churchill's remark that Indians would "breed like rabbits" famine or no famine, and wrote that he could see little difference between Churchill's outlook and Hitler's. Historians still divide over how the blame apportions, but the diary is a primary source.

The gold standard, 28 April 1925. As Chancellor he returned sterling to gold at the pre-war parity. He was not sure of it himself — in January he had circulated a memorandum putting sharp objections to the Treasury — and was talked round by Montagu Norman at the Bank of England and by the Treasury as a body. The result was uncompetitive exports, deflation, and the General Strike of 1926; he later called it the biggest blunder of his life. The lesson is finer than "he was wrong": he knew he did not understand, so he outsourced the judgement to the most authoritative people in the room — and those people shared a single blind spot.

Empire and power. As Secretary of State for War in 1919, on suppressing the Mesopotamian revolt, he wrote that he was "strongly in favour of using poisoned gas against uncivilised tribes" (he meant incapacitating agents, but the tone of the memorandum is itself the problem); in 1931 he publicly called Gandhi a "half-naked fakir". After his stroke in June 1953 he concealed his condition from the public and held on until 1955 before handing over to Eden, who had waited more than a decade.

Sources: Leo Amery's diary, September 1944; J. M. Keynes, The Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill (1925); P. J. Grigg, Prejudice and Judgment (1948) — the source for the "biggest blunder" remark; Churchill's War Office minute, May 1919.

◆What BigCat Can Take Away

One: locate the real fork. Most irreversible slides begin with a harmless-looking "let's just find out first"; in systems terms that is a commitment point, and it is worth marking before you reach it. Two: attention is a resource that has to be routed explicitly. The one-page rule, the ACTION THIS DAY tag, a day cut in half, rest by using a different set of muscles — he treated his own bandwidth as a system to be managed rather than a thing to be endured. Three: judgement is not a fixed personal attribute. The same certainty saved Britain in 1940, spent a quarter of a million lives in 1915, picked the wrong exchange rate in 1925, and shrugged at a famine in 1943. The question is not "is my judgement good" but "is this judgement of mine matched to the environment in front of me".

◆Questions Worth Sitting With

What would he be if he were born today?
His medium was a broadcast the whole country heard at the same moment. Today the same speech is cut into mutually contradictory fragments, and a construction like "the end of the beginning" — which needs three turns to land — does not survive its first reshare. His wartime power depended on battle reports that could be disclosed late; the colonial remarks, meanwhile, would be archived in full on day one. He would most likely be a formidable independent writer and a terrible keeper of wartime secrets.
Were the conditions for his success reproducible?
Mostly not. 1940 required three things at once: a parliamentary system still working well enough to hand power, in a crisis, to a man from the margins; a channel of water plus enough time to last until America entered; and the documented record of being right that his eight excluded years had bought him. The first two are exogenous. Only the third can be copied: when no one is listening, keep timestamping your judgement anyway.
What do his blind spots reveal?
His model of the world stopped somewhere around 1900: civilisation comes in ranks, and Britain's duty is to hold that order in place. The model has no slot for decolonisation, and no slot for the Bengal death toll either. Note that it is the same model that saved 1940: precisely because he believed Britain was the vessel of civilisation, negotiation was unthinkable to him. A person's strongest conviction is often also their largest blind spot — not two things, but two faces of one thing.
